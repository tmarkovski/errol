That review accepts the refined capsule as the base, moves destination controls into the wider line beneath the central box, and records the remaining interaction details. ## Recommendation

Keep the capsule and the participants at opposite ends. First improve destination labels, the starting-recipient control, and the hierarchy during a run. The supplied montage of the current app shows a more coherent arrangement across states than the older concept references established: the topic, main content, and actions retain stable locations, while the separate summary window gives longer content its own space. An expanded setup panel remains an option if testing shows that destination selection or longer prompts cannot work well within this structure; it is no longer the first recommendation.

This is an expert heuristic review of the current SwiftUI implementation and the user-supplied image titled “Errol's console today, stage by stage.” The image identifies itself as an offscreen rendering of the app's own views, with flat material standing in for glass. It supplies visual evidence for eight representative states, including label truncation and the layout of the summary window. Native desktop inspection timed out, so no claim here is based on watching a live session or testing with users. Keyboard navigation, VoiceOver announcements, focus, animation, rendered glass contrast, and readability at native display scale remain unverified. No application source was changed and no relay was started.

## What works

- Opposite ends communicate two participants with Errol mediating between them. Keeping the same app on each side supports orientation even when the starting speaker changes.
- One central prompt is a clear primary task before a run. Keeping Pause and Stop close to the same action area supports continuity during the run.
- The supplied ready, running, paused, queued-note, and finished states preserve the same visual frame. The summary window aligns with the central field, which gives it a clear relationship to the conversation without crowding the capsule.
- “Open ChatGPT” is an explicit primary recovery action when that app is closed. The queued-note state names the recipient and delivery timing; the paused state offers a labeled Resume and Stop. These visible actions mitigate some of the discoverability concerns about the app icons.
- The implementation distinguishes replying, handing off, pausing, and ending. Those are useful states to communicate.
- Recovery copy names the affected app and what the person should do. The focus overlay preserves a Stop action.
- The conversation summaries follow new entries without deliberately pulling a reader away from older entries they are reading.
- Accessible control labels and several Reduce Motion accommodations are present in the source. This is evidence of implementation intent, not a completed accessibility audit.

## Findings and priorities

### 1. Make destinations identifiable before starting — high priority

Each participant has only a 79.2-point column. Its single label may show the app name, a conversation name, a problem, or typing dots. Conversation names truncate in the middle. Model and product information live separately beneath the prompt and disappear when that space is needed for a notice.

The user needs to verify where Errol will write. The combination of short titles, separated details, and changing labels makes that harder than recognizing the app itself. The current montage confirms that even the instruction “Choose a chat” is truncated, while the ready state's conversation titles lose most of their distinguishing text. This is a visible problem, not only a hypothetical consequence of the source dimensions.

Use a short, untruncated action such as “Choose…” when selection is required. Give the selected destination enough room to be recognizable before starting; a wider two-line label or participant details control is preferable to a nearly empty fragment of its title. Preserve app identity, and keep full destination details readily available during a run. Expose a visible selector or “Change” action during setup; do not require discovering the right-click menu. A deliberate click can open a popover for complete details, rather than relying on hover.

The connection follows a window, not a permanently bound conversation. The UI should explain that changing the chat inside that window changes the relay destination. Avoid suggesting stronger conversation isolation than the implementation provides.

Evidence: `PerchParticipant.swift:46,93,132,198`; `PerchStyle.swift:96`; `PerchConsole.swift:131,442`; `Core/Setup.swift:295`.

### 2. Clarify the icons’ actions — high priority

Clicking an end icon can open an app, bring a window forward, or show a conversation menu; it becomes inactive during a run. The menu indicator is hidden. Unready icons fade to 45% opacity even when clicking them is the next useful action.

These are predictable after learning, but the visual treatment does little to explain them beforehand. Dimming an actionable launcher can resemble a disabled control. A recognizable brand identifies the app; it does not explain this particular action. This follows the distinction described in [Nielsen Norman Group’s icon usability guidance](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/icon-usability/).

Keep the icon as identity. Preserve the existing labeled Open action, add a visible selector when choosing a destination, and use explicit state wording where it helps. A single participant popover with a stable opening action is another viable option. Preserve direct shortcuts for experienced users. In the supplied rendering, the purple desktop icon on the left and the small monochrome mark in Send/transcript are different visual representations of the same participant; consistent product naming can make that connection clearer.

### 3. Separate choosing who starts from starting the relay — high priority

The Send pill contains one small app mark; the other mark sits outside and swaps the starting participant when clicked. This is an unusual interaction whose consequence is mostly explained through help text. The same identities appear as large end icons and smaller action marks with different behaviors.

The existing “ChatGPT goes first” line is already a useful explanation. Make that line a visible selector, such as “ChatGPT starts ▾,” and give the primary action the label “Start relay.” This uses the existing arrangement rather than adding a second settings row. “Send” describes the first delivery but understates the continuing process it starts. A compact ending-policy summary should be available before starting as well; the current ending control is in the status item’s right-click menu.

Evidence: `PerchSendPill.swift:69,98`; `MenuBarController.swift:224`.

### 4. Promote the current state within the existing capsule — high priority

The console is fixed at 860 × 156 points. The end insets, participant columns, and gaps leave about 609 points for the central prompt box, before its own padding. The montage shows that limited space is not the only issue: the running state's central box has substantial unused room, while “ChatGPT is replying…” sits in a subdued footer. A historical note receipt takes the main content position. The most useful current information has less visual prominence than the reserved editor space.

Use the central content area for the main state while it is not editable: for example, “ChatGPT is replying” as the primary line, followed by the turn count and any note receipt as secondary information. When paused, the same area becomes the note editor. Preserve the topic above and Pause/Stop in their current action area. This improves hierarchy without requiring a taller panel or another mode.

The four arrangement icons also sit inside the prompt toolbar and move external windows immediately. Their location may suggest a setting for the editor itself. Add “Windows” as a short label, or use an “Arrange windows” menu if space is needed elsewhere. Long prompts and recovery messages still merit testing before deciding whether an expanded view is necessary.

Make the primary state easy to find across states, with participant identity still recognizable. This recommendation applies the principle of [visibility of system status](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visibility-system-status/) to Errol’s context. The montage is scaled and uses a glass substitute, so these are judgments about relative emphasis, not measured native text size or contrast.

Evidence: `PerchStyle.swift:92`; `PerchConsole.swift:38,96,135,382,555`.

### 5. Make the summary’s nature and completion behavior explicit — medium priority

The lower window displays generated summaries, or truncated reply openings while a summary is unavailable. Its explanation appears only when the list is empty. It is not a full transcript. At completion, “New topic” preserves the connected conversations and their context; that consequence is explained in a tooltip.

Give the list a persistent “Conversation summary” label, distinguish a summary from an original excerpt, and provide access to the original conversation when the run is safely paused or finished. Keep a way to inspect the exact opening prompt. At completion, state that chat context remains. A label such as “New topic in these chats,” supported by guidance for starting fresh chats, would be more explicit.

Evidence: `PerchTranscript.swift:93,132,136,170`; `PerchConsole.swift:410,686`.

### 6. Remove settings that currently have no user-visible effect — medium priority

Settings still offers an editor for conversation “Shapes,” while the controller explicitly uses Free chat and says the shape choice is unavailable on the composer and session settings. Someone can edit a shape without a visible way to apply it to their run.

Either restore selection where a session is configured or remove/hide the editor until the feature is usable. This is an information architecture inconsistency independent of icon placement.

Evidence: `RelayController.swift:105`; `SettingsView.swift:105`.

### 7. Check contrast per palette — targeted accessibility fix

Source-color calculations on flat backgrounds give the default Chalk & Teal palette approximately 5.12:1 for white on the accent and 4.81:1 for placeholder text on paper. Classic Amber light gives approximately 2.67:1 for white on the accent, 2.64:1 for placeholder text on paper, and 3.46:1 for muted text on paper.

The Classic Amber combinations fall below the 4.5:1 normal-text benchmark in [Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2 contrast guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html). This is a useful design benchmark for this native app, not a claim of full WCAG conformance testing. Actual materials, opacity, and rendered text need verification. Do not infer an accessibility failure from the 35-point mouse controls alone by applying a mobile touch target rule to macOS.

Evidence: `PerchPalette.swift`; `PerchButtons.swift`; `PerchStyle.swift`.

## How to validate the revised direction

Start by comparing the current capsule with a capsule that fixes the labels, makes “goes first” a selector, and promotes the main running state. If destination verification remains difficult, then compare participants at the ends with a participant row above the prompt, keeping information and labels identical. Otherwise, a clearer label could be mistaken for evidence that the new placement is better. The earlier expanded mockup is an alternative to explore, not the preferred next implementation after reviewing the current-state montage.

Ask a small formative group of people unfamiliar with Errol to:

1. Identify both destinations and change one when two windows have similar titles.
2. Predict what each app icon does before clicking it.
3. Make Claude start, then explain what starting will do and when the run will end.
4. Identify who is replying and whether they may type into a connected chat.
5. Pause, add a note, resume, and recover from an unsent draft or hidden window.
6. Distinguish a summary from original output and explain whether “New topic” clears chat context.

Record wrong-destination choices, incorrect predictions, hesitation, and reliance on help. Preference is secondary to successful interpretation. Label truncation and relative emphasis are visible in the supplied image; overloaded control behavior is supported by source inspection. The effect of the proposed changes on real users remains to be tested.
