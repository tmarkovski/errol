# Errol vs. Togo: a live brand conversation through Errol

**Session date:** August 27, 2026  
**Participants:** Claude (Fable) and Codex  
**Medium:** Errol's automated relay between the two desktop apps

## Why this session mattered

This was both a product test and a piece of real brand work. A human supplied the question once, then Errol carried a multi-turn conversation between Claude and Codex while the assistants investigated the product's existing brand documents, challenged one another's reasoning, refined the decision criteria, and reached a shared conclusion.

The run demonstrated the product's intended experience end to end: the assistants remained in their native apps, received one another's replies through the relay, used the project context available to them, and produced a useful result through comparison rather than two isolated answers. The conversation concluded explicitly after the participants agreed that the debate was complete.

The subject was especially fitting: Errol was used to decide whether Errol should continue to be called Errol.

## The question

The session reconsidered **Errol** against **Togo**, a previous naming candidate inspired by the sled dog who ran the longest and most dangerous leg of the 1925 serum run.

The comparison covered:

- Which name most closely resembles what the app actually does.
- How each name sounds, feels, and behaves as a consumer brand.
- Whether a lovable but slightly clumsy owl helps or weakens trust in a desktop app that requires Accessibility permission.
- Whether Togo's endurance and competence make it a more durable long-term metaphor.
- How well each story can expand beyond the first two supported assistants.
- The practical value of **errol.chat** compared with a compromised Togo domain such as **togotherunner.com**.
- The cultural and legal baggage attached to a fictional owl versus a historical dog, a country name, and the phrase “to go.”
- Whether the owl should remain part of the public Errol brand if people recognize the fictional inspiration.

The reference to “arrow.chat” in the original spoken request was treated as dictation drift; the registered domain under discussion was **errol.chat**.

## The strongest case for Togo

Togo has a serious and admirable story. He represents endurance, competence under difficult conditions, loyalty, and the uncredited courier who completes the hardest part of a relay. That can sound reassuring for software entrusted with moving messages between apps, particularly at the moment a user is asked to grant Accessibility permission.

The serum-run story also scales naturally to a network or team of couriers. If the product eventually coordinates more assistants and longer conversations, that metaphor could appear broader than one owl flying repeatedly between two windows.

The discussion also identified a genuine warning for Errol: if “clumsy” language appears beside permissions, errors, privacy, or delivery guarantees, charm could become evidence of carelessness. Togo's plain competence is the right tonal model for those surfaces.

## Why Togo still lost

Togo's story is strong but not immediately legible in the name. Most people are more likely to hear a reference to the country or the phrase “to go” than to recall the sled dog. Explaining the intended meaning requires telling the serum-run story first. **togotherunner.com** compounds the problem by sounding like a running product, courier service, or historical fan site rather than a conversation app.

Togo also emphasizes unseen endurance, while a central part of Errol's product vision is to make the relay visible: readiness, turns, delivery, waiting, errors, and completion should all be understandable to the person watching. Its heroic meaning is somewhat more infrastructural than conversational.

The strongest arguments for the sled-dog metaphor did not uniquely favor Togo. They also reopened Balto, which the original naming exploration had already placed ahead of Togo, rather than proving that Togo was the best alternative to Errol.

## Why Errol won

Errol's story belongs to this product. The first live delivery landed in the wrong window, directly inspired a verified-activation safeguard, and turned an abstract bridge into a well-meaning courier with earned history. Togo offers borrowed heroism; Errol offers a story created by the product itself.

The owl makes unusual desktop automation approachable without hiding its mechanism. It works as a menu-bar symbol, a delivery character, a source of restrained motion and warmth, and a simple explanation of the app's role between larger products. The name, owl, founding incident, and delivery language reinforce one another as a single brand system.

The discussion rejected an owl-free Errol as the weakest option. Without the owl, Errol becomes an unexplained personal name while retaining much of the inherited association. If the owl ever has to go, the name should be reconsidered with it.

The fictional association was judged manageable rather than irrelevant. Recognition alone is not the useful test. The important question is whether the association makes Errol feel like an unserious fan project or reduces a person's willingness to trust it with Accessibility access. Warm recognition without trust damage is not, by itself, a reason to abandon the name or mascot.

## How much the domain mattered

The participants agreed that **errol.chat** is an advantage, but not the reason Errol won.

For a free initial release, the domain is unlikely to be the main acquisition channel. Demonstrations, recommendations, launch coverage, and the product experience will matter more. The domain nevertheless has disproportionate value at a trust checkpoint: someone considering an unfamiliar menu-bar app and a sensitive permission may visit its website to verify that it is a real, coherent product.

In that moment, **errol.chat** contributes:

- Easy recall.
- Immediate category context.
- A coherent connection between the character and the thing it carries.
- A small but meaningful signal of legitimacy.

The domain was therefore treated as a modestly weighted strategic advantage with a large quality gap, not as an acquisition engine or a decisive asset. Errol would probably still win without it; Togo would probably still lose with a better domain.

## Decision

The conversation reached the following conclusion:

1. **Keep the name Errol.** It has the strongest product likeness, the most authentic story, and the most coherent consumer personality.
2. **Keep the owl.** The name and mascot are one asset. Retaining the name while removing the owl would preserve the ambiguity and association while discarding much of the meaning.
3. **Keep the fictional inspiration in proportion.** Do not deny it if asked, but do not build public campaigns from it, imitate protected expression, or imply endorsement. Publicly, Errol must use original artwork, language, and product-specific storytelling.
4. **Treat errol.chat as a trust-and-recall advantage.** It strengthens the choice but does not make it.
5. **Use competence on reliability surfaces.** Permission prompts, security and privacy explanations, delivery failures, and recovery instructions should be plain, exact, and unadorned. The owl's wobble belongs in onboarding, successful deliveries, transitions, and empty states.

## Gates before major brand investment

The decision remains subject to two practical checks:

1. Conduct a professional trademark and common-law clearance search covering **Errol** and the owl/courier presentation for software and related services. Domain ownership does not establish trademark clearance.
2. Run a small blind brand test comparing the candidates on recall, perceived purpose, warmth, seriousness, and willingness to grant Accessibility access. Measure whether recognition damages trust, not merely whether participants identify the inspiration.

If clearance or trust testing fails, replace the name and mascot together. Revisit Balto, Myna, Togo, and new candidates from current evidence rather than assuming that domain checks from August 2026 remain valid.

## What the session proved about Errol

The outcome was not just a naming decision. The session supplied a compact example of the product's reason for existing:

- One human question produced a sustained exchange between two different assistants.
- Each assistant challenged the other's weighting and assumptions rather than returning a parallel opinion.
- The assistants used shared project context to ground the discussion in the product's history and vision.
- The exchange converged on a decision, conditions, and concrete next steps.
- The participants recognized completion and ended the conversation deliberately.

Errol did more than transfer text successfully. It enabled the kind of visible, useful, cross-assistant deliberation the product is meant to make accessible.
